01

The problem, stated precisely

What we are actually measuring, and why it is harder than it sounds.

Drowsiness is not visible in a single photograph. A frame showing closed eyes could be a blink — something a healthy driver does roughly 15 times a minute — or the first moment of a micro-sleep. The two look identical in one frame. They differ only in how long they last.

So the real task is not image classification. It is measuring a signal over time: track how open the eyes are on every frame, build a time series, and recognise dangerous patterns in it. That single observation drives the entire architecture below.

What a single frame gives us

  • Where the face is
  • Where the eyes and mouth are
  • How open they are, as a ratio

What only time gives us

  • Blink vs. micro-sleep (duration)
  • PERCLOS — closure over a minute
  • Blink rate and yawn frequency
02

The processing pipeline

Seven stages, run in order on every single frame. These are numbered because they genuinely are a sequence — each stage consumes the one above it.

1
Capture the frame
OpenCV · AVFoundation · background thread

The webcam is read on its own thread that keeps only the newest frame. If we read the camera on the main thread, we would drain a queue of stale frames and the display would lag behind reality — dangerous in a safety system.

out → 640×480 BGR image
2
Detect faces with YOLO
Ultralytics YOLO11n · fine-tuned on WIDER FACE

One forward pass produces every face box with a confidence score. YOLO — "You Only Look Once" — is a single-stage detector, which is precisely why it is fast enough for live video: no region proposals, no second pass.

out → face boxes + confidences · ~9 ms on Apple M2 GPU
3
Identify the driver
largest-area heuristic

With several people in frame we score the largest face box. A passenger sitting further back always projects to a smaller box, so this simple rule is both fast and reliable for a camera mounted in a car. Other faces are drawn in grey and labelled passenger.

out → one face box = the driver
4
Locate eyes and mouth
MediaPipe FaceMesh · 478 3-D landmarks

The face box is cropped (with a 15% margin) and FaceMesh runs on that crop only. Cropping first matters three ways: the eyes occupy far more pixels so landmarks are more precise, there is less image to process, and FaceMesh cannot wander off to a passenger's face — YOLO has already decided who the driver is.

out → EAR, MAR, eye boxes, mouth box, head tilt · ~5 ms
5
Score the drowsiness
stateful engine · 60-second rolling window

The only stage that remembers the past. It runs a small state machine that counts blinks, detects micro-sleeps and yawns, and blends four evidence channels into one 0–100 score. Detailed in section 04.

out → score, state, blink/yawn counters, PERCLOS
6
Render boxes and heatmap
OpenCV drawing · Gaussian attention map

Bounding boxes with class labels and confidences, an on-frame HUD, and the attention heatmap — whose intensity is computed from the live EAR and MAR, not painted on.

out → annotated frame + standalone heatmap view · ~7 ms
7
Sound the alarm
synthesised WAV · background thread · cooldown

If the state is DROWSY and has been for long enough, a two-tone siren plays on a background thread so the video never stalls.

out → audio, plus a flashing red dashboard banner
03

Eye Aspect Ratio

The core measurement — one number that says how open an eye is.

A bounding box cannot tell you whether an eye is open, because an open eye and a closed eye occupy almost the same rectangle. To measure eyelid closure you need the eyelid points. FaceMesh gives 478 of them per face, in real time, on the CPU.

From those we take six points around each eye and compute the Eye Aspect Ratio (Soukupová & Čech, 2016):

EAR = ( |p2−p6| + |p3−p5| ) / ( 2 · |p1−p4| )

Why divide by the width? That is the clever part of the formula. It makes EAR scale-invariant: when the driver leans toward the camera the eye gets bigger in pixels, but the gap and the width grow together, so the ratio is unchanged. Without the division we would need a per-person, per-distance calibration.

Blink or micro-sleep?

Both are "eyes closed", so the signal alone cannot separate them. We separate them by duration, measured at the moment the eyes reopen:

Closure lengthClassified asEffect on the system
≤ 0.40 sNormal blinkBlink counter +1. Score unaffected.
0.40 – 1.20 sHeavy blinkCounted as a blink; the closure channel starts rising.
≥ 1.20 sMicro-sleepForces DROWSY immediately and sounds the alarm.

Mouth Aspect Ratio

The same idea applied to the mouth: the average lip gap divided by the mouth width. A yawn is a large sustained MAR, so the mouth must stay open for 0.8 seconds before it counts. That duration requirement is what stops ordinary talking — which produces short MAR spikes — from being logged as yawning.

MAR = mean( |pupper − plower| ) / |cornerL − cornerR|

Three vertical lip pairs are averaged, which makes the measure robust to a single noisy landmark.

04

The drowsiness score

Four independent channels of evidence, weighted into one number from 0 to 100.

No single cue is trustworthy on its own. Someone can stare without blinking while perfectly alert; someone can yawn from boredom. So we combine four channels that fail in different ways, which makes the combination far more stable than any one of them.

score = 100 × ( 0.40·PERCLOS + 0.35·closure + 0.15·yawn + 0.10·blink )

Each channel is normalised to 0–1 before weighting, so no channel can dominate the others.

Why PERCLOS gets the largest single share

PERCLOS is the percentage of time the eyelids are closed over a rolling window — here 60 seconds. It is the measure that driver-fatigue research has repeatedly found correlates best with genuine drowsiness, because it captures the gradual lengthening of blinks that precedes sleep, rather than any single dramatic event.

Why "current closure" is nearly as heavy

PERCLOS is a 60-second average, so it responds slowly by construction. The closure channel is the opposite: it measures the closure happening right now and can take the score from 0 to alarm in just over a second. PERCLOS catches the slow slide; closure catches the emergency.

Safety override

If the eyes stay closed longer than the micro-sleep threshold, the state becomes DROWSY immediately, regardless of what the weighted score says. At 100 km/h a car travels about 33 metres every second — there is no time to wait for a rolling average to catch up. A weighted score is the right tool for gradual fatigue and the wrong tool for an emergency, so the emergency gets its own direct path.

Smoothing and the status bands

The raw score is exponentially smoothed — new = 0.25·raw + 0.75·previous — so the reading does not flicker between frames. The smoothed value maps onto three states:

ScoreStateMeaning
0 – 39ALERTNormal driving. Blinking within the healthy range.
40 – 59WARNINGEarly fatigue: longer blinks, occasional yawning.
60 – 100DROWSYAlarm sounds. Sustained closure or a micro-sleep.
05

The attention heatmap

Not decoration — every pixel of intensity is derived from a live measurement.

The heatmap answers the question "which part of the face is driving the score right now?". We place a two-dimensional Gaussian at each measured region:

G(x, y) = A · exp( −( (x−cx)² / 2σx² + (y−cy)² / 2σy² ) )

The amplitude A comes from the measurements — a lower EAR makes the eyes hotter, a higher MAR makes the mouth hotter, and the overall score drives a broad glow across the whole face. The width σ comes from the size of the detected region, so the blob always fits the feature it marks.

The blobs are summed, normalised, colour-mapped, and blended with a per-pixel weight rather than a uniform one. That detail matters: a flat blend would wash out the entire image, whereas weighting by local intensity leaves cold regions perfectly sharp and tints only what is actually hot.

Two optimisations worth mentioning

A naïve implementation evaluates the Gaussian over all 307,200 pixels for every blob. Instead we evaluate each blob only within ±3σ of its centre — beyond that a Gaussian contributes under 1% — and we colour-map at half resolution before scaling back up, since a heatmap is smooth by nature. Together these cut the cost from about 6.7 ms to 5.2 ms per frame, and to 0.2 ms when nothing is hot.

A fair question to expect

"Is this a real attention map, like Grad-CAM?" — No, and it is worth saying so plainly. Grad-CAM derives attention from a network's gradients. This map is built from the measured quantities the algorithm actually uses to decide: EAR, MAR and the score. It is an honest visualisation of the decision inputs, which is what a driver-monitoring display needs, but it is not a claim about what the neural network internally attends to.

06

The YOLO model

Which weights, why not the stock ones, and how to train a custom five-class detector.

Why stock YOLO is not enough

The Ultralytics models everyone starts from — yolov8n.pt, yolo11n.pt — are trained on COCO. COCO's 80 classes include person, but there is no face, no eye and no mouth. A stock model physically cannot produce the boxes this project needs, so there are only two honest options: use a checkpoint that was genuinely fine-tuned for faces, or train one.

What ships by default

models/yolov11n-face.pt — a real YOLO11n checkpoint fine-tuned on WIDER FACE (Apache-2.0, fetched from Hugging Face). It has exactly one class, face. YOLO locates the face; FaceMesh locates the eyes and mouth inside that box from 478 measured landmarks. Nothing is hardcoded — every box is computed from real landmark positions on every frame.

The custom five-class model

To have YOLO detect everything natively in a single pass, the project includes a complete training pipeline for five classes:

idclasswhat it marks
0facethe whole face
1eye_openan open eye
2eye_closeda closed eye
3mouth_openmouth wide open — a yawn
4mouth_closedmouth closed or nearly so

Auto-labelling: the interesting part

Hand-labelling thousands of boxes would take days. Instead the project uses weak supervision: a slow, complex teacher generates the labels for a fast, simple student. The YOLO-face model draws the face box, FaceMesh supplies exact eye and mouth contours, and the same EAR/MAR thresholds the live app uses decide open versus closed. The labels are therefore consistent with the criteria the finished system will be judged by. 

python training/auto_label.py --session normal   --frames 400
python training/auto_label.py --session eyesshut --frames 300
python training/auto_label.py --session yawning  --frames 300
python training/auto_label.py --split      # 75 / 15 / 10 split
python training/train.py --epochs 100 --install

Labels are written in YOLO's normalised format — class x_center y_center width height, every value between 0 and 1 — so they remain correct at any resolution.

A dataset trap we had to design around

Frames containing more than one face are skipped automatically. We can only measure eyes and mouth for one face per frame, so a second person's eyes would be left unlabelled — and in YOLO training, "unlabelled" means "background". The model would literally be taught that eyes are not eyes. This was caught by inspecting the rendered label previews, which is exactly why the --preview step exists.

Transfer learning, not training from scratch

Training starts from yolo11n.pt, which already learned generic visual features — edges, textures, "object-ness" — from COCO's 118,000 images. We re-teach only the final layers. That is why a few thousand of your own frames suffices, where training from random weights would need hundreds of thousands.

Augmentation chosen for this domain

Which metric actually matters here

Not overall mAP. For this application the number to defend is recall on eye_closed. A false alarm is annoying; a missed micro-sleep is a crash. The validation script reports it separately and warns when it falls below 0.90 — because an average across all five classes can look healthy while hiding exactly the failure that matters.

07

Engineering decisions

The choices behind the code, and what each one costs.

Why YOLO and MediaPipe together, rather than one of them?

They solve different problems. YOLO answers where is the face, and how confident am I — it is robust across lighting, angles and distances, and it gives a real confidence score for each detection. FaceMesh answers exactly where are the eyelids, which is what EAR needs and what a box cannot provide. Using either alone would mean giving up something the system genuinely requires.

Why crop the face before running FaceMesh?

Three reasons at once. Accuracy: the eyes occupy far more pixels in a 200×200 crop than in a 640×480 frame, so the landmarks are more precise and EAR is less noisy. Speed: less image to process. Correctness: YOLO has already decided who the driver is, so FaceMesh cannot drift onto a passenger's face.

Why read the camera on a background thread?

OpenCV buffers frames. If the processing loop is slower than the camera, reading on the main thread drains a queue of increasingly stale images and the display falls behind reality — unacceptable in a safety system. The background thread keeps only the newest frame, so we always process the present moment and simply drop frames we cannot keep up with.

Why play the alarm on a separate thread?

Playing audio synchronously would freeze the video for the duration of the sound — roughly a second of blindness, at exactly the moment the system has decided the driver is asleep. A background thread plus a cooldown timer keeps the video running while preventing hundreds of overlapping sounds during a sustained alarm.

Why synthesise the alarm instead of shipping an audio file?

The WAV is generated with NumPy on first run: two tones alternating six times a second, because hearing locks onto changing pitch far more readily than a constant tone. The frequency is integrated to get phase — otherwise the waveform jumps discontinuously at each switch and sounds like static — and a 12 ms fade at each end removes the click. It keeps the repository free of binary blobs, and the tone is tunable by editing numbers.

Why is every threshold in a YAML file?

EAR baselines differ between people, and the right threshold depends on the camera, the lighting and the individual. Hardcoding those numbers would make the system tunable only by editing source. The dashboard exposes the main thresholds as live sliders, so it can be calibrated in front of an audience without a restart.

What are the honest limitations?

Sunglasses defeat EAR entirely — no eyelid landmarks means no measurement. Very low light degrades both YOLO and FaceMesh; an infrared camera is the real-world answer. Extreme head rotation hides one eye. The largest-face heuristic assumes the driver is nearest the camera, which a rear-seat mounting would break. And the thresholds are population defaults, not per-driver calibration — a production system would learn each driver's personal EAR baseline during the first minute of a trip.
